## Abstract

We analyze intraday liquidity for a range of cryptocurrencies across different exchanges. Among the liquidity measures used, order book variation is most interesting for crypto traders, as it directly impacts their profit/loss. We find evidence that order book variation can be explained by liquidity measures indicating that trades are timed. We report various liquidity patterns that allow traders to increase their profits by minimizing liquidity-dependent trading costs. We further find indications that crypto exchanges can control liquidity by the number of offered currency pairs.

Keywords:

[cryptocurrencies](/search?q=cryptocurrencies); [liquidity](/search?q=liquidity); [market microstructure](/search?q=market+microstructure)

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, cryptocurrencies have grown significantly in prominence. An increasing number of investors now recognize cryptocurrencies as a distinct asset class. This recognition has been fueled by the emergence of altcoins, many of which have outperformed Bitcoin in recent years, thereby expanding the investment opportunities within this new asset class. These developments have spurred considerable research on cryptocurrencies, as evidenced by a rapidly growing body of literature. However, much of this research remains centered on Bitcoin and focuses primarily on data derived from price series or trades data, such as returns and volatilities. Liquidity, despite being of critical importance to cryptocurrency investors, has received comparatively less attention. A key reason for this is the limited availability of data; while price data are readily accessible and trade data rather easy to obtain, order book data are more challenging to come by. Most current papers focus on liquidity measures on the trades level, neglecting to a large degree that cryptocurrency markets are mostly still not mature enough to provide the necessary deep and developed order books on lower levels than best-bid and best-ask that is needed for professional traders.

From an academic perspective, liquidity and its evolution over time serve as important indicators of, and prerequisites for, market efficiency. From an investor’s perspective, liquidity directly affects transaction costs, which in turn influence the profitability of trading activities. An attractive trading platform provides the infrastructure necessary to trade (in our case, crypto) assets with high reliability and at low costs. This operational efficiency is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for achieving higher forms of market efficiency.

A critical component in this context is the total transaction cost, which can be divided into explicit and implicit costs. Explicit costs include, but are not limited to, trading, clearing and settlement fees for exchanges, as well as service and commission fees for market intermediaries. These costs are typically straightforward to measure and compare due to their transparency. Implicit costs and risks, on the other hand, such as those arising from order book (il)liquidity, are not as transparent and are often significantly harder to quantify. While the intuitive notion of a negative relationship between liquidity and implicit trading costs holds, it remains somewhat vague as it does not pertain to a specific concept or aspect of liquidity. Different facets of liquidity give rise to various liquidity measures. Simple proxies, such as trading volume, may offer a rough indication of liquidity but fail to capture its more nuanced and specific aspects.

In this paper, we examine multiple dimensions of order book liquidity of cryptocurrencies. Specifically, we explore variations in liquidity across a large set of very diverse currency pairs, different exchanges and over time. The insights derived from this analysis hold value for academics, investors, trading venues and regulators.

For investors, comparing the liquidity and associated transaction costs of currency pairs and trading venues provides valuable insights to inform decisions about what to buy, where to buy it and when to execute trades. Trading platforms can leverage these results to reassess their trading systems, market design and listings. For instance, they might consider introducing designated sponsors for liquidity provision, transitioning from a purely order-driven market to a hybrid market. These liquidity providers play a crucial role in order book markets, particularly when liquidity for certain trading pairs is weak—e.g., when spreads are excessively large or volumes at the best levels of the order book are insufficient.

The concept of 24/7 continuous trading might also warrant reevaluation. For currency pairs with low liquidity, auction models or continuous trading interrupted by auctions could be more effective in bringing together a larger pool of traders. Increased liquidity attracts more participants, ultimately boosting trading volume and the market share of the exchange.

Finally, our findings are also pertinent for regulators. Market competition is a common concern for regulators. In a consolidated (fragmented) market, there is low (high) competition among exchanges but high (low) competition among traders, resulting in low (high) liquidity costs and high (low) explicit transaction costs. Achieving the optimal degree of market fragmentation is critical to safeguarding investor protection. In the crypto world, fragmentation occurs not only across exchanges but also within a single trading venue, as these typically offer numerous trading pairs with different base currencies.

## 2. Literature

### 2.1. Liquidity in Order Books

Research on market liquidity began in the late 1960s with [Demsetz](#B33-jrfm-18-00124) ([1968](#B33-jrfm-18-00124)), who examined bid-ask spreads in dealer markets. The subsequent literature has produced influential contributions across theoretical (e.g., [Akerlof, 1970](#B1-jrfm-18-00124) for markets with information asymmetries; [Kyle, 1985](#B54-jrfm-18-00124) for the strategic behavior of insiders), empirical (e.g., [Huang & Stoll, 1996](#B52-jrfm-18-00124) on transaction costs in auction and dealer markets; [Christie & Schultz, 1994](#B26-jrfm-18-00124) on collusion in the NASDAQ dealer market), and experimental domains (e.g., [Plott & Sunder, 1982](#B63-jrfm-18-00124) on information dissemination in experimental auction markets; [Friedman, 1993](#B38-jrfm-18-00124) on experimental comparisons of auction and dealer markets).

The present paper relates to several strands of the literature. Research on cryptocurrencies in finance and economics is still at an early stage. The majority of papers in this area focus on the potential real effects of cryptocurrencies as payment and transaction mechanisms. [Ciaian et al.](#B30-jrfm-18-00124) ([2016](#B30-jrfm-18-00124)), [Harvey](#B49-jrfm-18-00124) ([2016](#B49-jrfm-18-00124)), [Böhme et al.](#B19-jrfm-18-00124) ([2015](#B19-jrfm-18-00124)) and [Raskin and Yermack](#B65-jrfm-18-00124) ([2018](#B65-jrfm-18-00124)) provide broad perspectives on the economics of cryptocurrencies and the underlying blockchain technology. [Pagnotta and Buraschi](#B61-jrfm-18-00124) ([2018](#B61-jrfm-18-00124)) propose valuation models for digital currencies. [Easley et al.](#B36-jrfm-18-00124) ([2019](#B36-jrfm-18-00124)) and [Huberman et al.](#B53-jrfm-18-00124) ([2021](#B53-jrfm-18-00124)) analyze bitcoin mining fees and the incentives of miners in equilibrium.

The literature examining cryptocurrency market liquidity has focused on centralized exchanges (CEX) or decentralized exchanges (DEX). While CEX employ limit order book markets, DEX usually use Automated Market Maker (AMM) protocols since limit order books are not feasable on-chain due to limited throughput and gas fees. [Barbon and Ranaldo](#B8-jrfm-18-00124) ([2021](#B8-jrfm-18-00124)) conducted a comparative study between the liquidity on centralized and decentralized exchanges and outline the rising competition of decentralized exchanges different concept of liquidity. [Lehar and Parlour](#B56-jrfm-18-00124) ([2025](#B56-jrfm-18-00124)) compare transaction-based liquidity measures between Uniswap v2 (DEX) and Binance (CEX). For CEXs, [Brauneis et al.](#B16-jrfm-18-00124) ([2021](#B16-jrfm-18-00124)) compare low-frequency and high-frequency liquidity measures for Bitcoin and Ethereum.

[Brauneis et al.](#B16-jrfm-18-00124) ([2021](#B16-jrfm-18-00124)) note that few studies utilize full order book data to examine the liquidity of cryptocurrency markets. [Dyhrberg et al.](#B35-jrfm-18-00124) ([2018](#B35-jrfm-18-00124)) investigate the trading dynamics of Bitcoin on US-based crypto exchanges, while [Makarov and Schoar](#B58-jrfm-18-00124) ([2020](#B58-jrfm-18-00124)) explore arbitrage opportunities across exchanges. In most cases, low-frequency measures have been employed without comparative frameworks. [Brauneis and Mestel](#B15-jrfm-18-00124) ([2018](#B15-jrfm-18-00124)) test the predictability of cryptocurrency returns, and [Al-Yahyaee et al.](#B2-jrfm-18-00124) ([2020](#B2-jrfm-18-00124)) study the efficiency of cryptocurrency markets, finding that inefficiencies are primarily driven by illiquidity and volatility in cryptocurrencies.

[Anand et al.](#B5-jrfm-18-00124) ([2005](#B5-jrfm-18-00124)), [Back and Baruch](#B7-jrfm-18-00124) ([2007](#B7-jrfm-18-00124)) and [Ranaldo](#B64-jrfm-18-00124) ([2004](#B64-jrfm-18-00124)) highlight the advantages of limit order books, which include, but are not limited to, low transaction costs (e.g., brokerage, commissions, spreads and fees), pre- and post-trade transparency, efficient price discovery through comprehensive information aggregation, reduced information asymmetry and a more competitive market microstructure.

The nature of liquidity provisioning in limit order book (LOB) markets differs significantly from that in quote-driven markets in one critical aspect. Limit order suppliers—i.e., the de facto market makers—face very low entry and exit barriers. They can freely provide or withdraw liquidity from the market based on prevailing market conditions. This contrasts with the designated market makers in quote-driven markets, who are obligated to provide liquidity. This key feature of liquidity provisioning in LOB markets is encapsulated by the “free-entry and free-exit” hypothesis proposed by [Brockman and Chung](#B18-jrfm-18-00124) ([2002](#B18-jrfm-18-00124)).

Previous literature has extensively documented the informativeness of limit order books and their role in reducing information asymmetry. [Cao et al.](#B20-jrfm-18-00124) ([2009](#B20-jrfm-18-00124)) examined the informational content of order book levels beyond the best bid and ask. Similarly, [L. E. Harris and Panchapagesan](#B48-jrfm-18-00124) ([2005](#B48-jrfm-18-00124)) find that limit order books provide valuable insights into future price movements. The state of the order book—characterized by tightness, depth and imbalance—can be assessed using various measures, such as spread, volume and impact costs.

### 2.2. Liquidity Measures in Order Books

Liquidity in limit order book (LOB) markets is a dynamic interplay between market orders, which consume liquidity, and limit orders, which supply it. Traders actively monitor the market and engage in competitive behaviors to optimize their trade execution by either demanding or supplying liquidity ([S.-L. Chung et al., 2012](#B29-jrfm-18-00124); [Foucault, 1999](#B37-jrfm-18-00124); [L. Harris, 1998](#B46-jrfm-18-00124); [Parlour, 1998](#B62-jrfm-18-00124)). This endogeneity in traders’ decisions reflects their attempts to adapt to prevailing market conditions. For instance, traders frequently undercut existing quotes to improve upon the best bid-ask prices, showcasing the competitive dynamics of liquidity supply and demand ([Biais et al., 1998](#B11-jrfm-18-00124)).

Impatient traders typically employ market orders to consume liquidity, while patient traders place limit orders, thereby supplying liquidity. These limit order traders are compensated for their patience and for providing the service of liquidity; however, they also bear execution risk and pick-off risk from informed traders ([Baruch, 2005](#B9-jrfm-18-00124); [Seppi, 1997](#B69-jrfm-18-00124)). The LOB structure efficiently matches liquidity suppliers with demanders, maintaining a balance that is critical for market operations.

The relationship between liquidity supply and demand is influenced by market conditions. Research has shown that limit orders increase significantly during periods of low liquidity, as indicated by widened spreads and other liquidity metrics ([K. Chung et al., 1999](#B27-jrfm-18-00124); [Griffiths et al., 2000](#B42-jrfm-18-00124); [L. Harris & Hasbrouck, 1996](#B47-jrfm-18-00124); [Hollifield et al., 2004](#B51-jrfm-18-00124); [Ranaldo, 2004](#B64-jrfm-18-00124)). Conversely, during periods of high liquidity, the proportion of market orders rises. This dynamic creates a mean-reverting behavior in bid-ask spreads and contributes to the ecological equilibrium in liquidity supply and demand within the market ([Handa et al., 2003](#B43-jrfm-18-00124), [1998](#B45-jrfm-18-00124); [Handa & Schwartz, 1996](#B44-jrfm-18-00124)).

The nature of liquidity provisioning in LOB markets contrasts sharply with that in quote-driven markets. In LOBs, liquidity suppliers face minimal entry and exit barriers, allowing them to adapt quickly to market conditions. This “free-entry and free-exit” characteristic, as described by [Brockman and Chung](#B18-jrfm-18-00124) ([2002](#B18-jrfm-18-00124)), is a defining feature of LOB markets. However, its implications for market resilience and reliability, particularly during crises, remain underexplored ([Anand & Venkataraman, 2016](#B6-jrfm-18-00124); [Tripathi et al., 2021](#B70-jrfm-18-00124)).

Recent studies have highlighted asymmetric behavior between the bid and ask sides of LOBs, particularly during crisis periods. For instance, ([Cenesizoglu & Grass, 2018](#B21-jrfm-18-00124)) found that the ask side deteriorates more significantly than the bid side during crises, contributing disproportionately to market instability. ([Sensoy, 2019](#B68-jrfm-18-00124)) also noted these asymmetries, emphasizing the need for separate analyses of the bid and ask dimensions of liquidity.

Most liquidity studies in LOBs focus on spread and depth, derived from price and volume information at the best bid-ask levels ([Chordia et al., 2000](#B23-jrfm-18-00124), [2002](#B24-jrfm-18-00124), [2008](#B25-jrfm-18-00124)). However, evidence from developed markets suggests that the best bid-ask orders may be noisy and less informative ([Amaya et al., 2018](#B3-jrfm-18-00124); [Cao et al., 2009](#B20-jrfm-18-00124); [Cenesizoglu & Grass, 2018](#B21-jrfm-18-00124); [Deuskar & Johnson, 2011](#B34-jrfm-18-00124)). Deeper levels of the order book often provide a more accurate assessment of liquidity, but accessing such data is challenging, particularly in emerging markets ([Bekaert et al., 2007](#B10-jrfm-18-00124); [Lesmond, 2005](#B57-jrfm-18-00124)).

The components of the bid-ask spread also differ significantly between dealer and LOB markets. In dealer markets, spreads primarily compensate designated market makers for inventory holding, information asymmetry and order processing costs ([Amihud & Mendelson, 1986](#B4-jrfm-18-00124); [Glosten & Milgrom, 1985](#B39-jrfm-18-00124), [Ho & Stoll, 1983](#B50-jrfm-18-00124)). In contrast, in LOBs, spreads reflect transaction costs, adverse selection costs and waiting costs borne by limit order suppliers ([Brockman & Chung, 1999](#B17-jrfm-18-00124); [Chan, 2000](#B22-jrfm-18-00124); [de Jong et al., 1996](#B32-jrfm-18-00124)).

The shape of the LOB, characterized by parameters such as depth, height, slope, resilience and elasticity, significantly impacts price formation, liquidity provisioning and volatility ([Cao et al., 2009](#B20-jrfm-18-00124); [Næs & Skjeltorp, 2006](#B59-jrfm-18-00124)). A thick order book with depth concentrated around the best quotes reflects a consensus on the true price and promotes stability ([Goettler et al., 2005](#B40-jrfm-18-00124); [Valenzuela et al., 2015](#B71-jrfm-18-00124)). Conversely, thin order books with dispersed depth indicate mispricing and increased short-term volatility ([Obizhaeva & Wang, 2013](#B60-jrfm-18-00124); [Roşu, 2009](#B66-jrfm-18-00124), [2020](#B67-jrfm-18-00124)). Asymmetries in the order book shape can signal higher trading costs and potential inefficiencies ([Biais et al., 1995](#B12-jrfm-18-00124)).

The resilience of the order book—its ability to recover from order flow shocks—is a critical yet underexplored aspect of market efficiency ([Degryse et al., 2005](#B31-jrfm-18-00124); [Large, 2007](#B55-jrfm-18-00124)). Recent advancements in high-frequency data availability provide opportunities to examine this property more closely, particularly during crisis events.

Future research could explore the resilience of LOBs in greater detail, particularly examining how free-entry and free-exit dynamics affect liquidity provisioning during market stress. Additionally, asymmetries between the bid and ask sides warrant further investigation to understand their implications for market stability. Finally, expanding the study of liquidity measures to emerging markets, where data limitations persist, could provide valuable insights into the global dynamics of liquidity.

This paper contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we focus on liquidity, whereas most prior studies on cryptocurrencies have analyzed other aspects. Second, instead of limiting our analysis to Bitcoin, we examine a broad set of altcoins. Additionally, we contribute to the sparse body of research that incorporates deeper levels of order books—considering prices and quantities beyond the best bid and ask—when assessing limit order book liquidity. Third, unlike order books from other markets, the full limit order books of cryptocurrency exchanges are unique in providing 24/7 trading data.

## 3. Data

### 3.1. Raw Data

Order book data from the exchanges Binance, Kraken, Huobi and OKEx were obtained through CryptoTick (<https://www.cryptotick.com/>, accessed on 6 August 2021). The sample period spans 1 January 2019, to 30 September 2019, covering 273 days. The data are provided as individual files for each currency pair, exchange and trading day, with dates and times standardized to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC).

The dataset includes all levels from both sides of the order book as made available to CryptoTick by the exchanges. While some exchanges report the full set of orders, others impose limitations. For instance, Binance reported only the top 20 bids and asks until mid-June 2019.

The time frame of the sample captures numerous new listings and delistings on the exchanges, with some currency pairs appearing or disappearing between January and September 2019. Additionally, certain trading days are missing from the dataset, likely due to suspended trading or unavailability of data for unknown reasons.

These 24/7 limit order book data enable the reconstruction of (parts of) the order books for evaluating the liquidity of currency pairs. Unlike traditional markets, where trading hours are limited, the continuous trading nature of cryptocurrency markets may shift or concentrate liquidity at specific times of the day. Notably, trading activity captured in our limit order books is uninterrupted by auctions, in contrast to systems like XETRA, which employs opening, intraday and closing auctions for German stocks.

### 3.2. Data Processing

Each file begins with an order book snapshot that lists all available price levels on both sides of the order book at the start of the day. Subsequent entries document updates to the current order book status. To facilitate analysis, we created 5-min order book snapshots containing up to 20 levels on each side (if available), resulting in 288 order book snapshots per day. The use of 5-min intervals is widely accepted in the literature as it represents a balanced compromise between computational efficiency and the retention of essential informational content.

To ensure data quality, we applied several filters. First, we excluded all days with order books reporting negative or zero bid-ask spreads at five-minute intervals. Such instances should have triggered the matching and removal of the corresponding orders from the order book. Second, we omitted all days with order books reporting negative quantities for any order, as this is economically nonsensical.

These issues indicate inaccuracies in the raw data. Since we could not identify where the errors in the raw data occurs, we omitted the entire day’s observations for that pair. Such omissions are rare in our sample and are unlikely to significantly impact our results. This filter applies to 2543 dates across 453 trading pairs (1392 on OKeX, 767 on Binance, 324 on Huobi and 60 on Kraken), less than 2% of the raw data, resulting in 254,728 observations across 1086 trading pairs.
